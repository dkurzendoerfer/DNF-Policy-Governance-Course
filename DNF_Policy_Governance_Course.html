This is a short orientation to Policy Governance, the framework our board uses to do its job. Policy Governance is a widely adopted model in which the board defines organizational outcomes (Ends) and boundaries (Executive Limitations) through written policy, delegates all operational authority to the General Manager, and evaluates performance based on whether the GM's interpretation of those policies is reasonable and supported by evidence.

The goal of this course is not to make you an expert, but to give you enough context that your first few board meetings feel familiar rather than foreign.

Module 1 of 5

Why do we govern this way?

The term Policy Governance can sound formal, but the underlying idea is straightforward: the board sets direction and boundaries, while the General Manager runs the store. We do not dictate which brand of oat milk to stock, or how to staff the deli on a Tuesday. Operational decisions belong to the GM.

Instead, the board writes policies that establish expectations, such as maintaining financial stability and treating staff fairly, and then reviews performance regularly to confirm those expectations are being met.

Member-Owners elect the board and provide ongoing feedback, including through the Member Comments topic that opens every board meeting, where directors share comments they have heard from members throughout the month. The board governs itself through Policies C & D, and directs the GM through Policies A & B. The GM runs the store, which in turn serves the community of Member-Owners.
The core principle

The board governs. The GM manages. We speak with one voice rather than seven. And we evaluate the GM based on results and compliance, not on whether each of us would have made the same choice individually.

This structure keeps the board focused where it belongs: on strategic leadership, long-term organizational health, and accountability to our member-owners. DNF has operated under this framework for years, and it is part of the reason our small co-op consistently performs beyond its size.

Module 2 of 5

The four kinds of policies.

Every policy in our manual falls into one of four categories. Together, they represent the four dimensions of a board's work. Please click each card below to see an example from DNF's actual policy manual, then continue.

A
Ends
The why. What we exist to accomplish in the community.
+
Example: Excerpt from Policy A (Global Ends)
From DNF's Policy Manual: Durango Natural Foods is a member-owned food cooperative committed to providing quality organic local products and educating our community on the value of healthy living. We:
  • Engage the community in cooperative principles
  • Participate in a sustainable local economy
  • Educate consumers about the broad impact of their choices
  • Provide tools for living a healthy lifestyle
  • Respect the environment through our actions
Ends policies define the outcomes the GM is expected to produce.
B
Executive Limitations
The guardrails placed on the GM. Actions or conditions the GM must not allow.
+
Example: Excerpt from Policy B1 (Financial Condition and Activities)
From DNF's Policy Manual: With respect to the actual, ongoing financial conditions and activities, the General Manager must not cause or allow the Cooperative to be unprepared for future opportunities, the development of fiscal jeopardy, or key operational indicators to be below average for our industry. The GM must not:
  • Allow sales growth to be inadequate
  • Allow operations to generate an inadequate net income
  • Allow liquidity to be insufficient
  • Default on any terms of the Cooperative's loans
These are just a few of the sub-points within B1. Notice how each item is framed as something the GM must not allow, rather than a specific instruction.
C
Board Process
How the board governs itself. Meetings, officers, committees, and conduct.
+
Example: Excerpt from Policy C4 (Directors' Code of Conduct)
From DNF's Policy Manual: Directors must demonstrate unconflicted loyalty to the interests of the Cooperative's owners. This accountability supersedes any conflicting loyalty such as that to advocacy or interest groups, membership on other Boards or staffs, and the personal interest of any director acting as an individual consumer or member.
C policies govern how the board conducts itself. This one establishes that a director's first loyalty is to the member-owners as a whole.
D
Board-Management Relationship
How the board connects to the GM. Delegation, monitoring, and accountability.
+
Example: Excerpt from Policy D3 (Delegation to the GM)
From DNF's Policy Manual: As long as the GM uses any reasonable interpretation of the Board's Ends and Executive Limitations policies, the GM is authorized to establish all further policies, practices and plans for the cooperative. The Board will respect and accept the GM's choices as long as those choices are based on reasonable interpretations of Board policies.
D policies define the connective tissue between the board and the GM. This one spells out the core delegation principle.

Most of what the board reviews during monthly meetings comes from the B policies, the Executive Limitations. These are numbered B1 through B8:

  • B1Financial Condition and Activities
  • B2Planning and Budgeting
  • B3Asset Protection
  • B4Membership Rights and Responsibilities
  • B5Treatment of Customers
  • B6Staff Treatment and Compensation
  • B7Communication and Support to the Board
  • B8GM Succession
Key concept: "any reasonable interpretation"

The phrase reasonable interpretation appears throughout Policy Governance. Here's what it means in practice:

What it is:

A reasonable interpretation is one that a prudent, informed person in a similar role would consider defensible when reading the policy. The GM must be able to articulate and justify the interpretation, and the board is the final arbiter of whether it meets that standard.

What it is NOT:
  • Whatever a director would have chosen personally
  • An interpretation the GM cannot explain or defend
A useful principle:

If the board is unhappy with an interpretation that is reasonable, the remedy is to rewrite the policy, not to override the GM. When policy language produces results we do not want, we tighten the language.

What the GM sends us each month.

In advance of every board meeting, directors receive a set of reports from the GM. Below is what to expect, and how to approach each one. Please click each tile below to view a real excerpt from a recent report, then continue.

B
Monthly B-Policy Report
One per month, rotating

Each month, the GM reports on one of the B policies. For each sub-point within that policy, the GM states whether the co-op is in compliance, explains the reasoning, and provides supporting evidence. Over the course of a year, the board cycles through all of them at least once.

  • 4. Allow inadequate security of premises and property. GM Reported: Compliance "The premises are protected by a security system which is functional, maintained and tested."

B1
B1 Financial Report and Narrative
Quarterly, with financial statements

Given the importance of financial oversight, B1 follows a quarterly cadence. Each quarter, directors receive the B1 compliance report, a financial narrative that walks through the balance sheet and profit and loss statement in plain language, and the financial statements themselves. Every director is expected to review these materials, with particular attention from the Treasurer.

M
Manager's Report
Every month

Unlike the B-policy reports, the Manager's Report is not a formal monitoring document. It is how the GM keeps the board informed about operations: department highlights, staffing updates, upcoming events, and anything else the board should know about daily activity at 575 E. 8th Ave.

How to approach these reports as a director

Your role is not to audit every number or second-guess every decision. The question you are answering is: Is the GM's interpretation reasonable, and is it supported by evidence? When something looks inconsistent or unclear, ask questions. When the reasoning and evidence hold up, accept the report and move on.

Module 4 of 5

Your role as a director.

A few principles to keep you grounded as you begin. We have grouped these into two categories: the governing principles that shape how the board operates, and the practical expectations for showing up well.

Governing Principles

  • 01
    One voice, not seven. The board deliberates in many voices but governs in one. Once a vote is taken, that decision becomes the board's position, regardless of how any individual director voted.
  • 02
    No individual authority over the GM. A single director cannot direct the GM. Authority rests with the board as a body, exercised through policies, formal motions, and official decisions.
  • 03
    Loyalty to member-owners. Not to outside advocacy groups, not to a favored department, not to personal shopping preferences. Your duty is to the full membership.
  • 04
    Confidentiality where appropriate. Some board discussions, such as personnel matters and the GM's compensation or performance review, are confidential. Directors are expected to respect that confidentiality and to continue honoring it after leaving board service.

Practical Expectations

  • 05
    Come prepared. This is the most important one. Before every board meeting, the GM and team send a package of materials, including the monthly B-policy report, the Manager's Report, and any supporting documents. Read through each of these beforehand so meeting time can be spent on meaningful discussion.
  • 06
    Serve on a committee. Beyond monthly board meetings, directors serve on one or more standing committees, such as the Finance Committee, Feasibility Committee, or Special Events Committee. Committees do much of the research and preparation work that supports the full board's decisions.

When uncertainty arises about what is appropriate, ask. The board has operated under this framework for many years, and every current director was once new to it. Questions are always welcome at this table.
